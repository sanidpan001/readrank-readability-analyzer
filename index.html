The complete guide to readability scores: Flesch-Kincaid, Gunning Fog, SMOG and Coleman-Liau

Every writer has had the same uneasy moment: you publish something you think is crystal clear, and readers bounce off it like a brick wall. A free online readability checker takes the guesswork out of that moment. Instead of asking a friend "does this make sense?", you paste your draft into a tool like ReadRank and get back hard numbers — a Flesch Reading Ease score, a Flesch-Kincaid grade level, and three more independent formulas — each one estimating how much education a reader needs to understand your text on the first pass. This guide explains what each of those scores actually measures, what "good" looks like for different audiences, and how to use a reading level checker to systematically make your writing easier without dumbing it down.

What a readability checker actually measures

Despite the scientific-sounding names, every mainstream readability formula is built from the same two humble ingredients: sentence length and word length. Longer sentences force readers to hold more clauses in working memory. Longer words — measured either in syllables or letters — slow down word recognition. Combine those two signals and you get a surprisingly good proxy for comprehension difficulty, validated over decades against real reading tests.

The Flesch Reading Ease score, developed by Rudolf Flesch in 1948, is the grandparent of them all. It runs from 0 to 100, where higher means easier. A score of 90–100 reads like a comic book; 60–70 is "standard" plain English; 30–50 is academic-paper territory; anything under 30 is borderline unreadable for a general audience. Most professional copywriters aim for 60–70, the band where roughly 80% of adults can follow along comfortably.

The Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level, created in 1975 for the US Navy, translates the same math into American school grades. A score of 8.0 means an average eighth-grader — roughly a 13-year-old — should understand the text. This is the number most people mean when they say "reading level," and it is the default grade shown by ReadRank's Flesch Kincaid calculator panel. For context, the average American adult reads at roughly a 7th–8th grade level, which is why "write at grade 8" has become the standard advice for web content, marketing copy, and public communication.

The other three formulas, explained

Gunning Fog Index, invented by textbook publisher Robert Gunning, adds a twist: it counts "complex words" (three or more syllables, excluding proper nouns and common inflections) and penalizes texts that are stuffed with them. The result estimates the years of formal schooling needed to understand the passage on first reading. A Fog score of 12 means a high-school senior should manage; a score of 17 or higher means graduate-level difficulty. Because it punishes jargon directly, Fog is the formula that reacts most dramatically when you swap words like "utilize" for "use" — which is exactly why ReadRank pairs it with a simpler-synonym list.

The SMOG Index (Simple Measure of Gobbledygook, coined by G. Harry McLaughlin in 1969) is built on polysyllable density alone. It takes the square root of the number of long words, scaled to a 30-sentence sample, and is widely regarded as the most accurate predictor for health and safety materials — the US National Institutes of Health recommends it for patient information. Its one quirk: it genuinely needs about 30 sentences to stabilize, so treat SMOG scores on short snippets as directional rather than precise. ReadRank still shows SMOG for any text length but flags short samples.

Coleman-Liau Index takes a completely different route: instead of counting syllables (which is fiddly for computers), it counts letters. Average letters per 100 words and sentences per 100 words feed a regression that outputs a grade level. It tends to be slightly harsher on technical writing full of long compound terms, and because it never needs a syllable dictionary, it is the fastest of the five to compute. When all five formulas agree — say, everything lands between grades 7 and 9 — you can be confident the score is real and not a quirk of one formula's math.

Why one score is never enough

Ask five different free readability checkers to score the same paragraph and you will often get five different answers. That is not because the math is wrong — it is because each formula weights the ingredients differently. A paragraph of short sentences packed with three-syllable jargon will score "easy" on Flesch-Kincaid (which leans on sentence length) but "hard" on Gunning Fog (which counts those long words). A paragraph of long, flowing sentences built from tiny words does the opposite. ReadRank shows all five side by side precisely so you can see these disagreements instead of being misled by one.

The practical rule: trust the consensus, investigate the outliers. If Flesch-Kincaid says grade 8 but Fog says grade 13, your problem is vocabulary, not sentence structure — swap the jargon. If Fog and Flesch agree but SMOG spikes, your polysyllable density is high and you need simpler word choices across the board. If Coleman-Liau is the odd one out, look for long compound words and hyphenated monsters. The per-sentence heatmap makes this diagnosis visual: the red sentences are where your worst habits cluster, and fixing just those often moves the whole-document score more than rewriting everything.

What is a "good" score? Target bands by audience

There is no universally correct reading level — only the right level for your readers. Writing for children aged 7–10? Aim for grade 5 or below, with Flesch Reading Ease above 90. Writing for the general web — blog posts, product pages, help docs? Grade 8 is the sweet spot, ease 60–70. This is the band most search-engine guidance implicitly rewards, because it matches how the broadest audience actually reads. News sites typically land at grades 6–10: skimmable, quotable, never a chore.

Business and B2B writing can run slightly hotter — grades 9–12 — because the audience is professionally motivated and the subject matter demands precision. But beware the trap: most corporate writing scores at grade 14+ not because the ideas are complex, but because the sentences are bloated. "Leverage synergistic frameworks to facilitate optimal outcomes" is a grade-16 way of saying "work together to get good results" (grade 6). Academic and technical writing for specialists may legitimately sit at grades 12–16, where domain vocabulary is shared shorthand rather than showing off. ReadRank's audience presets encode these bands so you can check your draft against the right target in one click instead of guessing.

How to lower your score without dumbing down your ideas

The single highest-leverage fix is splitting long sentences. A 40-word sentence at grade 14 becomes two 20-word sentences at grade 9 with zero change in meaning — and ReadRank's worst-sentence diagnosis shows you exactly where to cut. Look for the conjunctions: "and," "but," "which," "because." Each one is a candidate split point, and the tool previews the predicted grade of each half so you can see the improvement before you commit.

The second fix is vocabulary triage. You do not need to purge every long word — precision matters — but audit the ones doing no work. Latinate verbs are the usual suspects: "utilize" (use), "facilitate" (help), "commence" (start), "implement" (put in place), "approximately" (about). The simplify-words panel surfaces these automatically with plain replacements; swapping five or six per page routinely drops a full grade level. Prefer Anglo-Saxon roots over Latin ones, active voice over passive, and concrete nouns over abstractions.

Third, vary your rhythm. A wall of identical 18-word sentences scores fine on the formulas but reads like a metronome. Mix short punchy sentences with longer explanatory ones — the average stays low while the prose stays human. Finally, use the revision compare feature as a feedback loop: paste your rewrite, check the delta, and iterate. Writers who do three passes with a reading level checker typically cut two to three grade levels off a first draft while keeping every idea intact.

Readability and SEO: what actually matters

Search engines have never confirmed readability as a direct ranking factor, and any readability checker that promises "higher rankings" is overselling. But the indirect effects are real and measurable. Easier text keeps readers on the page longer, reduces bounce rates, and earns more natural shares and links — all signals that search engines do reward. Google's own content guidelines repeatedly emphasize writing for people first, in clear language, which is readability by another name.

There is also an accessibility angle that matters for both ethics and reach: roughly 15% of the world's population lives with some form of dyslexia or reading difficulty, and plain language is a core requirement of WCAG accessibility guidance. A grade-8 page is simply usable by more humans than a grade-14 page. Treat your readability score as an inclusion metric, not just a style metric, and the SEO benefits follow as a side effect of serving readers better.

Common myths about reading level checkers

Myth one: "a low score means the writing is bad." Nonsense — it means the writing is hard. A quantum physics paper at grade 16 can be excellent; a landing page at grade 16 is a conversion disaster. Always judge the score against the audience, which is why target bands exist. Myth two: "shorter is always better." Choppy, oversimplified prose can score grade 4 and still be painful to read; the formulas measure difficulty, not quality, and they cannot see tone, structure, or argument.

Myth three: "you need 300+ words for a valid score." Longer samples are more stable, yes, but the formulas work on any text — you just get more noise with less data. SMOG is the most sample-hungry of the five; Flesch-Kincaid and Coleman-Liau settle down much faster. Myth four: "readability formulas understand meaning." They do not. They count surface features that correlate with difficulty. A perfectly clear sentence with one unavoidable technical term can still score high. Use the numbers as a diagnostic flashlight, not a verdict — then apply your own judgment.

Frequently asked questions

What is a readability checker?

A readability checker is a tool that analyzes your writing and estimates how easy or difficult it is to read. It counts sentence length, word length, and syllables, then applies established formulas — such as Flesch Reading Ease or Flesch-Kincaid — to produce a score or grade level. Writers use it to make sure their text matches their audience, from children's books (grade 5 and below) to general web content (around grade 8).

How is the Flesch-Kincaid grade level calculated?

The Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level uses the formula: 0.39 × (words ÷ sentences) + 11.8 × (syllables ÷ words) − 15.59. The result maps to US school grades — a score of 8.0 means an average eighth-grader should understand the text. Longer sentences and longer words both push the grade higher, which is why splitting sentences and swapping long words are the two fastest ways to lower it.

What is a good Flesch Reading Ease score?

Flesch Reading Ease runs from 0 to 100, where higher is easier. Aim for 90–100 for young readers, 60–70 for general audiences (the "standard" plain-English band), and 50–60 for fairly difficult material. Scores below 30 indicate very difficult, academic-level text. Most professional copywriters target 60–70 because that band is comfortable for roughly 80% of adult readers.

What is the difference between Gunning Fog, SMOG, and Coleman-Liau?

All three output a grade level but measure different things. Gunning Fog counts "complex words" (3+ syllables) and estimates years of schooling needed — it punishes jargon hardest. SMOG uses polysyllable density alone and is considered the most accurate for health and safety materials, but needs about 30 sentences to stabilize. Coleman-Liau counts letters instead of syllables, making it fast and slightly stricter on technical terms. ReadRank shows all three so you can trust their consensus.

How accurate is an online reading level checker on short text?

Short samples produce noisier scores because one unusual sentence swings the averages. As a rule of thumb, 100+ words gives reasonably stable Flesch-Kincaid and Coleman-Liau scores, while SMOG really wants around 30 sentences. For short snippets, focus on the per-sentence heatmap rather than the document totals — it shows exactly which sentences are hard regardless of sample size.

How can I improve my readability score quickly?

Split your longest sentences first — one 40-word sentence cut in two can drop a full grade level with no meaning lost. Then replace Latinate verbs with plain ones: "utilize" → "use", "facilitate" → "help", "approximately" → "about". Finally, prefer active voice and concrete nouns. Use ReadRank's worst-sentence diagnosis and revision compare to measure each pass; most writers cut 2–3 grade levels in three editing rounds.
